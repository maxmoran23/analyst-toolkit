## Background

A stablecoin is a digital asset designed to hold a stable value, almost always by reference to a fiat currency — most commonly the US dollar — and most often backed by a reserve of assets held against the tokens in circulation. By 2022 stablecoins had become the settlement layer of crypto markets: the dominant pair on exchanges, the principal collateral in decentralized finance, and a fast-growing rail for cross-border value transfer. They had achieved this scale largely outside any purpose-built regulatory regime, sitting awkwardly across existing categories of money-transmission law, e-money law, securities law, and banking law depending on the design and the jurisdiction.

That ambiguity became untenable in May 2022, when a large algorithmic stablecoin — one that attempted to hold its peg through an arbitrage mechanism with a paired token rather than through a reserve of assets — lost its peg and collapsed. The episode did not just destroy the algorithmic token; it crystallized a policy view that some stablecoins were structurally fragile, that the label "stable" could be misleading, and that the run-risk and contagion concern was real. The period from 2022 to 2026 is, in large part, the story of regulators and legislators converting that view into rules. This article traces that evolution across the major markets and reconciles where the public record is settled against where it remains contested.

## How the Window Opened: The 2022 Shock and the Pre-Existing Patchwork

Before 2022, stablecoin oversight was a patchwork. In the United States, stablecoin issuers were generally regulated — to the extent they were regulated at all — as money transmitters under state law, a regime built for remittance businesses rather than for entities issuing dollar-pegged instruments at scale. Federal bodies engaged through guidance, supervisory statements, and enforcement rather than a dedicated statute. Internationally, jurisdictions with e-money frameworks could sometimes fit certain stablecoins inside them, but the larger reserve-backed tokens often did not map cleanly onto any existing category.

The May 2022 collapse changed the salience of the issue rather than the legal landscape itself. International standard-setters had already been studying the question — the Financial Stability Board (FSB) and the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) committee structures had been developing recommendations on what they termed "global stablecoin arrangements" [1]. The 2022 event lent those workstreams urgency. The FSB went on to publish high-level recommendations for the regulation of global stablecoin arrangements, emphasizing that arrangements posing comparable risks to existing payment or financial activities should face comparable regulation — the "same activity, same risk, same regulation" principle that recurs throughout the period [1].

The interpretive consensus that formed after 2022 had three parts. First, that an asset calling itself a stablecoin should actually be backed by assets — algorithmic stabilization without a reserve drew the most skepticism. Second, that holders needed an enforceable right to redeem at par. Third, that issuers needed to be identifiable, supervised entities rather than diffuse protocols. The rest of the window is the working-out of those three propositions into binding law.

## The European Union: MiCA as the First Comprehensive Regime

The European Union produced the period's most complete answer. The Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA) — agreed in 2023 and published in the EU's Official Journal — created a single, harmonized framework for crypto-assets across the bloc, and within it a specific regime for stablecoins [2]. MiCA does not use the word "stablecoin" as its primary legal category; it splits the instruments into **asset-referenced tokens** (ARTs), which reference a basket of assets, currencies, or crypto-assets, and **e-money tokens** (EMTs), which reference a single official currency [2].

Several features of the MiCA stablecoin regime are notable as a template other jurisdictions were measured against:

| Element | MiCA approach (as set out in the Regulation) |
|---|---|
| Issuer eligibility | Issuance restricted to authorized entities — for EMTs, credit institutions or authorized e-money institutions |
| Reserve backing | Reserve assets required, segregated from the issuer's own assets, with composition and custody requirements |
| Redemption | A legal right of redemption at par for holders |
| Scale controls | Additional requirements and usage limits for tokens that reach a "significant" scale, reflecting systemic-risk concern |
| Supervision | A role for the European Banking Authority alongside national competent authorities, with enhanced oversight of significant tokens |

A widely noted feature of the MiCA rollout is that the stablecoin provisions (the ART and EMT titles) applied **earlier** than the rest of the framework — the regime was sequenced so that the stablecoin rules took effect first, with the broader crypto-asset service provider provisions following [2]. This sequencing signaled that stablecoins were viewed as the more pressing systemic priority. By the close of the window, MiCA's stablecoin regime had become the most-cited reference point in international debate: a binding, in-force, reserve-and-redemption regime covering a major economic bloc.

## The United States: From Enforcement to Legislation

The United States travelled a different path: slower, more contested, and structured around the country's distinctive dual federal/state system. For most of 2022-2023, US stablecoin oversight continued to run through state money-transmission licensing, federal supervisory engagement with bank-affiliated issuers, and enforcement. The policy debate, however, moved decisively toward dedicated legislation.

The substance of the US debate concentrated on a recognizable set of design questions:

1. **The federal/state split.** Should a stablecoin issuer be chartered and supervised federally, by a state, or under a system that allows both with a federal floor? This is the most distinctively American question, and proposals through 2024-2025 generally contemplated a path for both federally supervised and state-supervised issuers, with conditions [3].
2. **Who may issue.** Whether issuance should be limited to insured depository institutions and their affiliates, or open to a broader class of licensed non-bank issuers, was a central and contested line.
3. **Reserves.** Proposals converged on requiring full backing in high-quality, highly liquid assets — cash and short-dated government instruments — with restrictions on rehypothecation and requirements for segregation.
4. **Redemption and disclosure.** A clear redemption right and recurring public disclosure of reserve composition, often with an attestation or audit requirement, featured consistently.
5. **Yield.** Whether a payment stablecoin may pay interest or yield to holders — which bears directly on whether the instrument competes with bank deposits and whether it looks like a security — was repeatedly contested.

Over 2024 and 2025, stablecoin-specific legislation advanced through the US Congress, with the legislative process — committee action, floor consideration, reconciliation between chamber versions — being the principal venue where the design questions above were fought out [3]. Because the precise final form, effective dates, and the allocation of authority among federal agencies depend on the enacted statutory text and subsequent rulemaking, this article does not state them as settled; that is flagged in Open Questions. What can be said from the public record is directional: the US moved from a posture of applying pre-existing law through guidance and enforcement toward a posture of purpose-built statute, and the center of gravity of the debate was institutional design rather than the core prudential controls, on which a rough consensus already existed.

## Other Major Markets: Convergence on Controls, Divergence on Design

Beyond the EU and US, several jurisdictions advanced stablecoin-specific regimes over the window, and the pattern is consistent: convergence on the prudential controls, divergence on the institutional architecture.

- **United Kingdom.** The UK legislated to bring certain cryptoassets, including stablecoins used for payments, within the regulatory perimeter, with HM Treasury, the Financial Conduct Authority, and the Bank of England developing the detailed regime — the Bank of England taking a particular interest in systemic payment stablecoins [4]. The UK approach distinguishes systemic from non-systemic arrangements and routes the former toward central-bank oversight.
- **Singapore.** The Monetary Authority of Singapore developed a stablecoin-specific regulatory framework focused on single-currency stablecoins, setting expectations on reserve quality, capital, and redemption for tokens that meet defined criteria [5].
- **Other markets.** A number of additional jurisdictions in Asia, the Middle East, and elsewhere advanced licensing regimes or designated stablecoins within broader virtual-asset frameworks over the period.

The cross-jurisdictional comparison can be summarized:

| Dimension | Observed cross-jurisdictional pattern, 2022-2026 |
|---|---|
| Reserve backing | Strong convergence — full backing in high-quality liquid assets is near-universal in the new regimes |
| Redemption at par | Strong convergence — a legal redemption right is a near-universal feature |
| Segregation of reserves | Strong convergence — reserves separated from issuer assets and bankruptcy-remoteness emphasized |
| Disclosure / attestation | Convergence — recurring reserve disclosure expected, though audit vs. attestation rigor varies |
| Who may issue | Divergence — bank-only, e-money-institution, and licensed-non-bank models all in use |
| Supervisory architecture | Divergence — single regulator, twin-peaks, and federal/state models all in use |
| Algorithmic stablecoins | Convergence toward restriction — uncollateralized algorithmic designs face the hardest path everywhere |
| Treatment of yield | Unsettled — no clear cross-jurisdictional consensus on whether holders may receive yield |

## The Systemic-Risk and Monetary Lens

A distinct thread running through the window is the view of stablecoins from a financial-stability and monetary-policy standpoint, advanced by central banks and international bodies. The concerns are recognizable from money-market-fund and payment-system policy: run risk (a sudden loss of confidence triggering mass redemption), the quality and liquidity of reserves under stress, contagion into the assets a large issuer holds, operational and settlement risk in the payment function, and — for tokens referencing a foreign currency — the risk of currency substitution in economies with weaker currencies [1]. International standard-setters' recommendations reflect these concerns, and the "significant" or "systemic" token concepts in MiCA and the UK regime are the direct regulatory expression of them: above a certain scale, a stablecoin is treated less like a payment product and more like systemic financial infrastructure [2][4]. Central-bank interest in stablecoins also intersects with central-bank digital currency work, though the two are distinct policy tracks and this article does not conflate them.

## Where the Sources Disagree

The contradiction pass surfaced several genuine tensions in the public record:

- **Is a payment stablecoin a deposit, a security, an e-money instrument, or a new category?** Different legal frameworks answer differently by construction. MiCA's EMT category leans on the e-money lineage [2]; some US debate framed payment stablecoins as a distinct non-deposit, non-security category, while other voices argued certain designs resemble securities or deposits [3]. This is not a factual disagreement to be resolved by recency — it is a genuine policy choice that different jurisdictions made differently, and the article presents it as such rather than picking a "correct" answer.
- **Federal vs. state primacy in the US.** Commentary across the period disagreed on whether state regimes should be preserved with a federal floor or substantially federalized. The public record shows the question was contested through the legislative process; the resolution depends on enacted text and is left to Open Questions.
- **Effective dates and the "first comprehensive regime" claim.** Sources broadly support that MiCA's stablecoin provisions were sequenced to apply ahead of the rest of the framework [2], which underpins the description of the EU as moving first on a comprehensive, binding basis. Precise application dates should be confirmed against the Regulation's own text and any implementing measures; the article states the sequencing fact and avoids asserting exact dates it cannot here cite.
- **Whether algorithmic stablecoins retain any compliant path.** The post-2022 direction is clearly restrictive, but sources differ on whether collateralized or hybrid algorithmic designs could qualify under the new regimes versus being effectively foreclosed. The honest reading is that uncollateralized algorithmic designs face the hardest path and that the status of collateralized variants is jurisdiction- and design-specific.

## Open Questions

The evidence base, as of the research date, leaves the following genuinely unresolved:

- **Final US statutory architecture.** The enacted form of US stablecoin legislation — the precise federal/state allocation, the agency rulemaking that follows, effective dates, and the eligibility perimeter for issuers — cannot be stated definitively from this article's sources and would require the enacted text and subsequent rulemakings to confirm.
- **Extraterritorial reach and cross-border interoperability.** How regimes treat a stablecoin issued under one jurisdiction's rules but used by residents of another, and whether equivalence or recognition arrangements emerge, is unsettled.
- **The yield question.** Whether, and under what conditions, a regulated payment stablecoin may pass yield to holders — and how that interacts with deposit and securities law — lacks a cross-jurisdictional answer.
- **The deposit / money-market-fund boundary.** Where exactly a reserve-backed payment stablecoin sits relative to a bank deposit and a regulated money-market fund remains contested, with implications for which body of law governs.
- **Effect on issuer concentration and market structure.** Whether comprehensive regulation consolidates issuance among a few large, well-capitalized (often bank-affiliated) issuers, and what that means for competition and resilience, is an empirical question the window's data does not yet answer.
- **Algorithmic designs.** Whether any algorithmic or hybrid design has a durable compliant path remains open and design-specific.
